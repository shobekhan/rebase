# Doing all the things

- You constantly choose what to work on. As a staff engineer, every request (incidents, mentoring, side projects, etc.) is a decision about where to invest your limited time.
- Not everything should become your responsibility. Learn to decide what is worth owning.
- Consider both company value and personal value. Don't only ask, "Is this good for the company?" Ask, "Is this good for my career and well-being?"
- It's okay to prioritize yourself. Choosing work that improves your growth, reputation, and happiness isn't selfish—it's necessary for long-term effectiveness.
- Time is your most limited resource. Everyone gets the same 168 hours per week, so spend them intentionally.
- Manage more than just time. Six key personal resources need protection:
   - Time
   - Energy
   - Quality of life
   - Credibility
   - Social capital (relationships and trust)
   - Skills
- The importance of each resource changes over time. Career stage, recent successes, and personal circumstances affect which resources deserve the most attention.
- Every project has a cost and a benefit. Evaluate how a new commitment affects each of your resources.
Choose the right level of involvement. You don't always need to fully own a project—you can decide the appropriate "engagement shape."
- Optimize your decisions.
   - Increase the positives.
   - Reduce the negatives.
   - Say no when a project isn't worthwhile.
- Core principle: There is infinite work, but only one of you. Protect your finite resources by making deliberate choices.


### Energy

- Time alone isn't enough—you also need enough mental energy ("smartbrain") to do meaningful work.
- Some tasks have an energy threshold—if you're mentally drained, starting or completing them becomes very difficult
- Mental fatigue reduces quality, making it harder to:
   - Focus
   - Learn and retain information
   - Notice subtle details
   - Write or solve problems
- Different activities affect people differently.
   - Some people gain energy from meetings.
   - Others find meetings exhausting.
   - Know what energizes and drains you.
- Identify your high-energy tasks (e.g., coding, writing, debugging, reading) and schedule them when you're mentally fresh.
- Understand your personal energy patterns so you can plan your day effectively.
- Personal life impacts work energy.
   - Poor sleep
   - Caring for a baby
   - Moving house
   - Illness
   - Stress
   - all reduce the mental energy available for work.
- You can't completely separate work and personal energy. You bring the same energy level into work that you have outside of it.


### Credibility

A staff engineer must balance high-level thinking with hands-on technical understanding. If they become too disconnected from real engineering problems, others may lose trust in their decisions.

Credibility is built by:

- Solving difficult problems.
- Showing strong technical judgment.
- Staying open-minded instead of blindly promoting one technology.
- Communicating clearly and behaving professionally.
- Helping bring order to confusing situations.

Credibility is lost when:

- You appear disconnected from reality.
- You force your preferred solutions everywhere.
- You communicate poorly or create unnecessary drama.
- You fail to handle difficult situations responsibly.

### Social Capital

Social capital is the trust, relationships, and goodwill you build with other people that make them willing to support you.

It grows when you:

- Help others achieve their goals.
- Build strong relationships.
- Deliver on your commitments.
- Support people without always expecting something back.

It decreases when you:

- Frequently ask for favors but never help others.
- Fail on important commitments.
- Ignore relationships.

As a staff engineer, social capital is important because people are more likely to support your ideas and initiatives when they trust and respect you.
